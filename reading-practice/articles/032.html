What Scotland is getting wrong on recycling – and Wales is getting right

2026-11-16 · 958 词

Scotland has ambitions to become an environmental leader, but its recycling rate has just fallen to a five-year low.

The Scottish household recycling rate fell from 44.3% in 2024 to 42.8% in 2025, according to recently released figures. These figures also show the levels vary widely across the country, with Glasgow one of the lowest local authorities at only 24% and Clackmannanshire at 56%. The Scottish government had previously set a recycling target of 70% of waste by 2025.

It is tempting to blame Scottish households. But research has shown recycling is shaped by social, cultural and structural conditions. Participation improves when services fit people’s everyday lives and make recycling convenient. People need authorities to provide suitable local infrastructure, services and behavioural support to enable effective recycling.

Scotland’s recycling services remain inconsistent and confusing. This is partly down to the continuing variation between local authority systems. Depending on where someone lives, there can be differences in which materials are accepted, container types and colours and whether glass is collected at the kerbside.

The wide variation between councils, from 18.2% to 56.5%, points to inconsistent services rather than individual attitudes alone.

Meanwhile, Scotland has struggled to introduce a return scheme for drinks containers, where a deposit is paid and then returned when recycled. Initially expected to start in October 2025, this has been postponed to October 2027, and will no longer include glass, due to wrangles with the UK government over devolved powers.

This was expected to raise recycling levels of eligible drinks containers to around 90%. A similar deposit scheme in Ireland has been very successful at pushing up recycling levels.

What Wales gets right

UK-wide figures also show a distinct difference between the four nations: Wales recycled 58.6% (up from 4.8% of household waste in 1998-99), Northern Ireland 50.1%, England 43.8% and Scotland comes bottom at 42.7% in 2024. These outcomes reflect political choices, not simply how diligently households sort their rubbish. Scotland’s lower rate reflects lack of access to convenient collection systems, cost barriers, limited reprocessing capacity and increasing incineration of recyclables.

Overall, recycling has stalled across England, Scotland and Northern Ireland while incineration of waste has expanded.

The municipal rate of recycling has increased to 68.4% in 2024-25. Municipal waste includes all local-authority-collected waste, including some commercial waste, street sweepings and litter bins.

But Wales shows that stagnation in recycling levels is not inevitable, and the nation has become a global leader on this front.

This reflects a sustained Welsh government policy, reinforced in 2021 with a new approach that set statutory local authority targets, invested in collections, as well as monitoring and political backing. Wales also imposed a moratorium on new large-scale energy-from-waste developments in 2021.

The Welsh system is not perfect. Its municipal rate remained below the 70% national target for 2024-25. Nevertheless, 20 of its 22 local authorities improved, demonstrating what sustained institutional pressure can achieve. Its experience suggests that targets work best when they are backed by infrastructure, accountability and stable long-term policy.

Recycling history

The first UK recycling in the early 2000s focused on relatively accessible materials such as paper, glass, metals and garden waste.

But further progress depends on addressing food waste, plastic films, textiles, electrical products and complex packaging.

These streams are harder to collect, separate and process, while markets for some recovered materials, where waste is reused, remain uncertain. Variable quality, volatile prices and weak demand for some materials increase costs and risk, potentially leaving councils without buyers.

Another reason recycling have not climbed as high as predicted is that people cannot recycle materials that councils do not collect. To help people recycle more it is important to eliminate confusing labels, redesign packaging that was never designed to be recycled or create new markets for recovered materials.

Burning not burying

It’s important to note that landfill reduction is also an important environmental achievement, and in Scotland, for instance, landfill use has declined – down 82.5% since 2011 – although incineration has increased. In 2024 1.86 million tonnes was incinerated, an increase of 239,000 tonnes (14.7%) from 2023. Poorly managed landfill sites can pollute soil and water, release methane and require monitoring long after closure. The 1999 EU Landfill Directive, landfill tax, statutory targets and kerbside collections helped transform the system.

But the UK is increasingly burning what it no longer wants to bury. In 2024, 63 energy-from-waste plants burned 16.8 million tonnes of residual waste. Since 2024, there are ten incinerators under construction in England and two in Scotland, but none in Wales and Northern Ireland. As people recycle more there’s less need for these, so why are so many being built?

For non-recyclable waste, modern incineration may be preferable to landfill. It reduces waste volume and can generate electricity or heat. But it is controversial. Incineration is the dirtiest way the UK generates power. It emits carbon and causes smells as well as destroying materials that could otherwise remain in use. It also often causes nearby residents to worry about health risks.

In 2024, 82.3% of Scottish household waste classified as “other diversion from landfill” was managed through incineration. Landfilled household waste fell by 37.7%, but the quantity managed through “other diversion” rose by 16.1%. Scotland is therefore managing its residual waste differently, but without achieving an equivalent improvement in waste prevention or recovering or reusing materials.

The next waste revolution

The UK’s reduction in how much waste it deposits into landfill was the first waste revolution. The next change must be preventing waste and keeping products and materials in use for longer.

Governments should ensure residual waste contracts do not penalise councils for reducing rubbish. They should reward durable, repairable and genuinely recyclable products. Priority should be given to food, flexible plastics, textiles, electrical equipment and complex packaging.

Wales shows that sustained recycling progress is also possible. The next waste revolution will begin when conserving materials takes priority over finding another way to destroy them. Those in Scotland who want the country to take an environmental leadership role should take note.
